Week 3 – Time management and strategies- Self-regulated learning (SRL)
Date created: 06/05/2026
3. Self-managed learning
Description

In the discussion week 3 we focused on Self-Regulated Learning (SRL) using the scenario How the Alex Case Improved, and how students can manage their learning more effectively. I played a supporting contributor during the discussion, we explored common time thieves that reduce productivity. Some examples we identified were responding immediately to phone notifications, helping peers before completing our own tasks, studying only in short bursts, and starting assignments the night before the deadline. 

We also discussed misconceptions about time, such as thinking there will always be more time later or believing that working under pressure at the last minute is effective. Through the Alex case, we recognised how these habits can negatively affect academic performance. 

We realised that these behavior often lead to poor study routines and cause students to feel overwhelmed with their academic work. 

Reflection

Analysis
This discussion helped me understand that many students struggle with symptoms of poor time management. For example, feeling constantly behind with tasks and saying “there’s never enough time” are common signs that time is not being used effectively. Distractions such as notifications can interrupt concentration and reduce productivity. When students respond to every message immediately, it breaks their focus and makes studying less efficient. Studying only in short bursts without proper planning can also make it harder to understand and remember information. Another important issue is leaving assignments until the night before they are due, which creates stress and often leads to rushed work. These habits show that poor planning and lack of control over distractions can negatively affect learning.

Insights
From this case, I learned that self-regulated learning is about taking responsibility for our own learning process. It involves planning study time, setting priorities, and monitoring our progress. I also realised that small habits, such as checking phones frequently or delaying important tasks, can waste a lot of time. Understanding these behaviours helped me see why some students feel that they never have enough time. Developing better study habits and managing distractions are important steps toward improving productivity and academic performance.

Future Actions
In the future, I will focused of self-regulated learning strategies in my studies. I plan to reduce distractions by limiting phone use while studying and focusing on one task at a time. I will also start assignments earlier instead of waiting until the last minute. Creating a daily to-do list and setting clear study goals will help me organise my time better. By practicing these strategies, manage my time more effectively and improve my overall learning experience.

Date created: 06/05/2026
5. Responsible AI use
Description

In Lab week 5- we learn about psychological safety and professional feedback; as a communicator I engaged in different scenarios that required giving and receiving constructive feedback with our team . One of the most challenging scenarios for our team  was giving feedback to a senior colleague (hierarchy tension). I found it difficult to balance being respectful while still questioning assumptions, such as unsupported data and unrealistic timelines. I was also involved in peer-level discussions where I had to challenge ideas without dismissing others’ effort. These activities helped me reflect on how I communicate in group settings. We visited students art exhibition during our break. Peer feedback by interest by using open-ended questions is very effective, as it encourages discussion and helps others feel valued.

Reflection

 Analysis 

I noticed that I tend to default to directive or evaluative language when I feel under pressure or when I strongly believe an idea is not effective without filtering  the right words For example, I might say “this won’t work” or “you should change this,” which can sound judgmental and reduce psychological safety. This happens especially when there are time constraints or when I feel less confident, such as when speaking to someone more senior. These habits can unintentionally discourage open discussion and limit collaboration within the team. 

 Insight 

Through the dissection   I learned that constructive feedback is more effective when it focuses on support, clarification, and extension rather than judgement. Using phrases like “Can you help me understand your thinking?” or “What might happen if we tried…?” allows the speaker to maintain ownership of their ideas while still encouraging improvement. I also realised that constructive feedback plays a key role in building trust and group learning. When team members feel safe, they are more likely to share ideas, take risks, and engage in meaningful discussions, which leads to better outcomes. 

 Future Action 

 I learn for future discussions, I will practise using more open and respectful language, especially in challenging situations. I will focus on asking more questions in deep to understand before making judgments and framing feedback as suggestions rather than instructions and giving my opinion directly. For example, I will use phrases like “I wonder if we could explore…” or “How does this align with our target audience?” Additionally, I will be more aware of maintaining psychological safety by acknowledging others’ efforts before offering feedback. This will help me contribute to a more collaborative and trusting team environment.

WEEK 6. Responsible AI Use
Date created: 06/05/2026
6. Teamwork and collaboration
Description


In discussion of  AI bias is when an artificial intelligence system produces unfair, inaccurate, or unequal results for certain groups of people. This happens because AI learns from data created by humans, and if that data contains bias or lacks diversity, the AI will repeat those patterns. For example, some medical AI systems have been shown to perform better on lighter skin tones due to limited training data. Similarly, AI image generators often represent scientists as white men because that is what appears more frequently in online data. In class, we also explored how wording can influence meaning. For instance, different sentence structures can change how a situation is interpreted, showing how language itself can introduce bias. 

Reflection

Analyse – Why is AI bias a problem? 
AI bias is a serious issue because it can lead to unfair and harmful outcomes. In areas like healthcare, biased AI can result in misdiagnosis or unequal treatment, especially for underrepresented groups. This can increase inequality and reduce trust in technology. AI bias can also reinforce stereotypes by limiting how certain groups are represented in media and data. Ethical AI is important because it ensures fairness, accountability, and safety. While AI systems include safeguards to prevent harmful or inappropriate outputs, these rules are still based on human design, meaning bias can still exist within the system. 

Learning Insight – How might AI bias affect you personally?

 I use AI to help me brainstorm ideas, organise my work, and improve my English writing skills because I noticed that sometimes I provide incorrect or misleading information. This can affect the quality of my work if I rely on it too much. I also learned about the Socratic method, where questions are used instead of direct answers to encourage deeper thinking. This helped me realise that I should not depend entirely on AI and must critically evaluate its responses. 

Future Action – How will you reduce AI bias in your work? 
 I will use the PACE framework (Prompt, Answer, Check, Evaluate) to guide my AI use. in future and I will  be carefully write prompts, check the accuracy of responses, and evaluate the information before using it. I will also verify information using reliable sources and make sure to edit and review AI-generated content myself. By thinking critically and applying the Socratic approach, I can reduce bias and ensure my work is accurate and fair.

WEEK 7. Responsible AI Use
Date created: 06/05/2026
7. Critical thinking
Description

 On Monday, we watched a video about the first robot Sophia and discussed how AI is controlled by humans and how it is useful in our daily lives, as it can work 24 hours a day, 7 days a week without rest, unlike humans. We then discussed a scenario about the use of AI and whether responsibility lies with the user or the AI if rules are bypassed. We explored different perspectives on how AI should behave when handling appropriate or inappropriate and restricted prompts, and whether it is ethical for users to try to “trick” AI into giving answers. Each group member contributed their ideas, and we also compared how different AI tools respond to similar prompts, including ChatGPT, Google Gemini, and Microsoft Copilot.My role in week 7 was communicator

Reflection

Analysis:
In our group, we were mostly aligned on the idea that users should take responsibility if they intentionally try to bypass AI safeguards. We agreed that AI systems are designed with safety rules, and attempting to trick them goes against responsible use. When we tested the three AI tools, they generally gave similar answers. However, when we changed the way we asked the questions, Google Gemini provided more detailed information along with a warning. Some group members believed that if AI fails to prevent harmful or restricted content, then the responsibility should also fall on the AI system or its developers. Others argued that AI does not have awareness or decision-making ability, so the responsibility should remain with the user. These differences came from how each person viewed AI, either as a controlled tool or as a system that should fully enforce its own safety rules.

Learning insight:
 We learned that AI cannot think or understand like humans. It only generates responses based on patterns and data created by humans. This made us realise that using AI without thinking can lead to over-reliance, which may negatively affect learning, also learned that using AI responsibly is important. I noticed that ChatGPT is more friendly and easier to use, while Gemini and Copilot can sometimes provide more detailed but more formal responses.

Future Action:
I will use AI more carefully and intentionally and focused on using it to support my understanding rather than to replace my thinking. I will be more  mindful of how I phrase my prompts and make sure I am still doing the work myself. If AI refuses to respond, I will take it as a sign to reflect and ensure that I am using it responsibly and ethically.

Description

We learn about Social media  who can support mental health by connecting people with shared experiences across cultures. However, some users follow trends without thinking critically, so feedback helps reveal benefits and risks. Technology has made communication faster and cheaper, but over reliance may reduce face-to-face interaction and practical skills. Younger generations are sometimes viewed as less resilient, showing less patience at work and relying more on parental support than previous generations.

This reflection is based on Advait Sarkar’s argument that overreliance on AI tools can weaken critical thinking, creativity, and memory. He claims that while AI improves efficiency, it encourages users to outsource thinking and become passive. During the activity, my group discussed Sarkar’s ideas, identifying similarities and differences in how we viewed the impact of AI on thinking and learning.

Reflection

Analysis

Sarkar’s argument is convincing because it reflects real behaviours we observed in our group, such as trusting AI answers without questioning them. However, his evidence is mostly based on examples rather than strong research data. His argument depends on assumptions that users rely on AI uncritically and that reduced effort always leads to weaker thinking. Our group agreed that these risks exist, especially when AI is overused, but also noted that Sarkar does not fully consider how actively engaged users may still think critically while using AI.

Insight

Team discussion, gained that AI itself is not the main problem; rather, the way people use it determines its impact. Some group members highlighted that AI can support thinking by explaining concepts, generating ideas, and presenting multiple perspectives. This alternative perspective made me realise that Sarkar’s argument is somewhat one-sided and could be strengthened by acknowledging positive uses of AI.

Future Learning

This activity changed how I view AI in my own learning. I plan to use AI more critically by questioning its outputs, checking information, and thinking independently before accepting answers. I will treat AI as a support tool instead of a replacement for my own thinking, ensuring that my critical thinking skills continue to develop.And also by focusing on one clear idea to make it more coherent.

Week 13 Discussion Board – Using information ethically
Date created: 06/18/2026
13. Using information ethically
Description

Description 

We learned this week that ethical information use goes far deeper than avoiding copy-paste. Through researching to find the IT topics, paraphrasing sources, find the cite by building APA reference lists, and peer reviewing another group's work, I learned that genuine paraphrasing requires completely reconstructing ideas in your own voice — not simply swapping words. I also learned that patch writing, which I had unknowingly practiced before, is considered academically dishonest even when unintentional. Discovering credible sources through Google Scholar and library databases, rather than relying on Wikipedia, also strengthened my research foundation.

Reflection

Analysis 

The most difficult challenge was paraphrasing without patch writing. I found myself unconsciously mirroring the original sentence structure even when changing individual words. The technique of writing from memory after reading the source was transformative ,it forced my brain to reconstruct the idea rather than reproduce it. This single strategy changed how I approach source material entirely.

My understanding of citation also shifted significantly. I previously viewed it as a bureaucratic requirement. Through discussion and feedback during the session, I came to understand that citation is an act of academic transparency — it allows readers to verify ideas, trace original thinking, and judge the quality of research. APA formatting is therefore not just a style rule but a tool for intellectual honesty.

Insight – What I Learned from Reviewing Another Group's Work

Reviewing another group's paraphrases and reference list was unexpectedly eye-opening. Spotting patch writing and formatting inconsistencies in their work immediately made me more self-critical of my own. I realised that errors are far easier to recognise in others' writing, which is precisely why peer review is such a valuable learning tool. The feedback exchange — identifying two strengths and one improvement — also developed my ability to engage constructively rather than simply critically.

Future Application – Where I Will Use These Skills as an IT Professional

As an IT professional, Honest, Transparent, Engaged with idea of others I will regularly put it in my write technical reports, cybersecurity analyses, project proposals, and research documentation. In these contexts, accurately representing others' ideas without misrepresenting or plagiarising them is both an ethical and professional obligation. Fields like AI, data privacy, and cloud computing rely heavily on credible, well-referenced research. Applying proper citation demonstrates professional integrity and research credibility — qualities essential in any technology-driven workplace. These skills are not just academic; they are foundational to my future career.

When I write carelessly, it can make the other person feel like their time does not matter to me. Thinking about the reader first makes a big difference.

The discussion helped me understand what makes an argument strong or weak. A weak argument just states an opinion with nothing to back it up, for example: "Universities should not monitor students because it is unfair." A strong argument gives reasons and evidence, for example: "Universities should not monitor students because it invades their privacy and research shows it makes students less willing to participate freely in learning." The difference is that a strong argument gives people a real reason to agree with you.

Description:
We discussed 2 imported ideas in this week : the environmental and social cost of AI and the role of effort in learning. AI has a large hidden footprint, including energy use, water consumption, and pollution, and these costs are often paid by distant or vulnerable communities. This relates to the concept of externalised cost, where users benefit while others carry the burden. The video “The Case Against AI” explains “friction starvation,” which is the removal of effort and challenge due to technology. Dr. Sealey highlights that struggle is necessary for learning because of neuroplasticity, the brain’s ability to grow through effort and practice. She also warns that overusing AI can lead to outsourcing thinking, which weakens our ability to learn independently.

Reflection

Analysis:
Friction starvation happens when we avoid effort. In my experience, I used AI to generate assignment answers instead of solving them myself. Although this made tasks faster, I could not remember or explain the content later, showing weaker understanding. Neuroplasticity suggests that when we stop doing hard things, our brain development slows. For example, when I rely on AI for coding solutions, I skip important steps of thinking and problem-solving, which are essential skills in computing.

Convenience is not always beneficial. Once, I used AI to generate code quickly, but later I struggled to complete a similar task on my own. However, when I worked through and put the effort for coding step by step, even though it was more difficult, I understood the logic better and gained confidence. I also reflected on AI’s environmental impact. Knowing that my AI use contributes to energy consumption and pollution made me realise that harm still exists, even if I cannot see it directly.

Insight:
I realised that avoiding effort may feel easier, but it reduces my ability to learn deeply. Real understanding comes from struggling, making mistakes, and solving problems. AI is helpful, but it becomes harmful when it replaces my thinking instead of supporting it.

What I Will Do in the Future:
I will attempt my work independently before using AI and use it only for guidance, such as checking answers or getting hints. I will also be more mindful of how often I use AI to avoid overuse. This will help me strengthen my problem-solving skills and prepare for my career. For networking, I will follow cold outreach rules: research one specific detail, keep messages under 100 words, and ask one clear question.
